#### Designing for Satisfaction
**A prototype for a game where the main mechanic is to clean a house.**

More than *House Flipper*(2018), *This Bed We Made*(2023), or any other number of games where you clean or complete repetitive tasks, it was *The Beginner's Guide* (2015) that inspired me to develop ideas around the mechanic of cleaning in games. Though cleaning in that game is incredibly simple (a matter of pressing a button to complete an action, similar with all other mechanics in the aptly named *The Beginner's Guide*) those simple actions paired with the game's narration on the importance of cleaning made the game feel very relaxing, even meditative. The short game where you clean makes up a fraction of *The Beginner's Guide*, but it made me see cleaning both in and out of games in a new light.
So. How do you take something like a *chore*, and make it fun?

The first rule: design for satisfaction, not frustration.
All games have a push and pull evident in their difficulty levels- "fun" is found in making mechanics that are challenging, inching along *towards* frustration but not getting to the point where the player throws up their hands and quits the game entirely. Like editing in films, a lot of this can be fixed with a deft hand designing around and accommodating the needs of the player in a way that feels effortless. Make the *game* challenging, not the UI.

This is one such prototype made in RPGMaker MV. I wanted to have the player have access too and able to use their **items**. RPGMaker MV's native way of doing this requires an action which opens the item menu, an effective albeit slow and clunky method of doing so. In traditional RPGs where you're expected to be slow and thoughtful, executing incredible complex actions due to a buildup of strategy, it's an effective method. But for the style of games I wanted to make, it was too slow.
This original prototype was effective, but only due to the limited nature of it. Planned to be a demo for a horror game, I liked the player's quick access to items but could quickly see it becoming more and more unreasonable as more items were added. For a game including puzzle solving mechanics, the player might have any number of keys, tools, or other items. As a result, this system would be fun but quickly become unwieldy.

The next prototype. I took inspiration from games like *Minecraft*, *Stardew Valley*(2016) and the Dark Souls series and how they have their inventory on screen and you can rotate between a number of selected items.

The final prototype. This prototype I stripped away a lot of the story and other art elements and focused solely on the mechanics. I limited the number of items you could rotate through and designed them to account for a wide number of cleaning types. I made up a special "spray" that counts as a general cleaner, a wipe, a vacuum for cleaning the floor and a glove for grabbing and throwing things away manually. It will take the player a little bit of thought to what tool to use to clean what mess, but hopefully the way it's designed they can access those tools quickly and easily. Not a very complicated system, but perhaps enough for the player to feel satisfied.
