## Survive Together, or Fall Apart Alone
**Seamlessly integrating Narrative Design with Mechanics in an unreleased project**

This unreleased project was inspired by Supermassive Games' "interactive film" horror series in featuring permanent character death and having the story continue without them. The way Supermassive Games' projects play out has many different ways to play the game, numerous endings and character states, as well as a high graphical quality using motion-capture of real actors and film-like cinematography to immerse players in a movie-like framing. Characters can die and this affects the overarching narrative and limits decisions the player/s can make later.
**This project wanted to hone in on that quality.**

## The Challenge
Character death does have a real effect on Supermassive Games' games, but due to the complex nature of them there's a bit of a delayed reaction. Other characters do not always realize when another character has died outside specific scripted scenarios. Characters can only die in very specific planned moments, some characters having only one major possible death and being largely spared for the rest of the game. The game's mechanics don't change much if a character dies, and character deaths may strongly affect some characters but not others.

This project was planned with a much more limited scope than Supermassive Games. The first being the style of the graphics, which of course is difficult to achieve for a single game developer on their own. Another key component that necessitated a limited scope was the game's goal- **build a group of characters who are mechanically and narratively dependent on one another, and alter both gameplay style and narrative if one or more characters die.**

Each character has a unique relationship with each other. The more characters there are **compounds** this narrative and mechanical importance. As well, there was a need to account for how the overall narrative of the game would change as the characters live or die, outside of their unique arcs. As such, the more levels there are the more chances characters have to die, and again **the more the complexity of the game grows**.

The first goal then is to pin down- how many characters? How many levels? Then the specifics- the overarching plot (tweaked to fit the number of levels) and the characters themselves (their unique relationships and how they each affect the mechanics of the game).

## The Design
I settled on three characters, 9 levels. I cheated a bit- the first three levels work as a tutorial, so the characters can't die during them. This helps to reduce the complexity but also adds a lot of forgiveness early on, while players are still learning the mechanics of the game. Narratively, it makes sense too- these three tutorial levels help flesh out each character, their strengths and weaknesses, as well as the context of the game.

Designing the mechanics was interesting, and more or less simple. This game involves various puzzles and minigames, but most commonly three types. Each character starts with a strength in one minigame and weaknesses in the other, and are automatically assigned the minigame to their strength as the game progresses. **Therefore if a character dies, the remaining characters are trapped completing minigames which they are at a disadvantage in**, dramatically increasing their chance of a fail-state and therefore another death. This game also borrows inspiration from the 1989 role-playing video game *Sweet Home*; each character has a unique item which helps them solve puzzles. If a character dies the item is lost, and majorly affects which ending the player can receive.

The characters themselves were more challenging to design narratively. Like with the mechanics, each one were assigned character traits which could be either "positive" or "negative". Each of their unique relationships were also designed to be interlocking- each person sees one other character favorably, and one other character more negatively. None of them are mutual in their feelings, and thus the loss of even one member damages the relationship of the group and leads the remaining to be more fractured as a result. This in turn affects the endings of the game not only in who remains alive, but also how their character is shaped by the losses they did or did not undergo.
